# WGS-Urban_Rural_UR-BCM

Geography and intestinal parasites may jointly shape the gut microbiome, yet their relative 
contributions remain unclear, especially in underrepresented areas, such as Latin America.
Using molecular detection for nine intestinal parasites, we sampled Colombian children from
urban Medellín (n=48) and rural Unguía (n=32), who were classified as parasite-negative,
monoparasitized, or polyparasitized. MetaPhlAn 4 and HUMAnN were used for species-level
taxonomic and functional profiling. Medellín participants had higher Shannon diversity than
Unguía participants (P=0.0046). Geography was the strongest determinant of community
structure (PERMANOVA pseudo-F=4.998, P=0.001), although regional dispersion also
differed (PERMDISP P=0.0002). Ascaris lumbricoides and Trichuris trichiura detection were
associated with community composition (P=0.010 and P=0.013, respectively). Whereas
Giardia duodenalis, Blastocystis species, and polyparasitism were not. Dispersion differed by
Trichuris status (P=0.030). Neither individual parasite presence nor polyparasitism
significantly altered alpha diversity, and parasite loads were not correlated with diversity
indices. Differential taxonomic signatures included both characterized and unclassified
species-level genome bins. Functional profiling identified Ascaris as the only parasite
associated with metabolic differences after false-discovery-rate correction, involving lipid and
sulfur metabolism. These findings indicate that geography is the dominant ecological correlate
gut microbiome variation in this cohort, while parasite-associated effects are agent-specific.
